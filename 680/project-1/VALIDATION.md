## Issues Found

### 1. [Severity: **High**] [NEW] The sup-F break is at **2019**, not 2020 — and once the search is accounted for, no break is significant

Figure 4 Panel B sweeps Chow F-statistics across candidate break years. Replicating it:

| Break year | F | naive *p* | N₁ / N₂ |
| --: | --: | --: | :-- |
| 2018 | 2.374 | 0.096 | 17 / 7 |
| **2019** | **3.5224** | 0.0303 | 18 / 6 |
| 2020 | 3.4301 | 0.0331 | 19 / 5 |
| 2021 | — | — | degenerate |

The **maximum of the trajectory the paper plots is 2019**, a pre-pandemic year. The narrative reports the 2020
value and concludes:

> "A statistically significant structural break occurs in 2020 (F = 3.430, p = 0.033), confirming that the
> COVID-19 pandemic and the structural emergence of hybrid telecommuting permanently altered the baseline
> elasticity relationship."

That is the *second*-highest point. A QLR/sup-Wald procedure estimates the break date as the argmax — which is
2019 and cannot be attributed to COVID.

Worse: applying the correct critical value for a *searched* maximum (Andrews 1993 sup-Wald, k = 4, 15% trimming:
≈ **4.09** at 5%) rather than the ordinary F critical value, **sup-F = 3.52 is not significant**. The single
"Significant (p < 0.05)" row in the Code Cell 7 table and the entire Figure 4 conclusion both dissolve.

**Impact:** reverses a stated finding. The paper's only claimed statistically significant structural break does
not survive correct inference, and the break date it does report is not the one its own data selects.

**Fix:** report sup-F = 3.52 at 2019, state it is below the Andrews 5% critical value, and conclude no
significant break is detected. This is also more consistent with the notebook's own Chow table, where every other
candidate year is merely "marginal."

### 2. [Severity: **High**] [NEW] `transit_vrm_total` — the RQ2 regressor — has a 28% average unallocated residual

Modal components do not sum to the totals in any year:

| Check | Result |
| :-- | :-- |
| `transit_vrm_bus + transit_vrm_rail` vs `transit_vrm_total` | short in **25/25** years, by **18.0% to 36.4%** (mean 27.7%, sd 5.0pp) |
| `transit_upt_bus + transit_upt_rail` vs `transit_upt_total` | short in **25/25** years, by 4.8% to 5.2% |

The data dictionary lists Vanpool and Paratransit as in-scope modes, so a residual is expected — but no vanpool or
paratransit column exists in the 68-column CSV, the residual is nowhere documented, and it is **not a stable
share**: it drifts 18% → 36% → 26% across the panel.

This matters because RQ2 asks about *"regional transit capacity (transit vehicle revenue miles)"* and the
Abstract frames the result as being about *"regional transit fixed-guideway service."* The variable actually
regressed is roughly one-quarter to one-third vanpool and paratransit — demand-response and commuter-match
services that deliver no corridor congestion relief and whose revenue miles move for reasons unrelated to
fixed-guideway capacity. A meaningful part of the γ = +0.698 coefficient is being generated by a mode the
research question does not cover, and the drifting share means the measurement error is not even constant.

**Fix:** re-run the cross-modal model on `transit_vrm_bus + transit_vrm_rail` (or rail alone) and report it
alongside. Add the missing mode columns to the CSV, or document the residual explicitly in the data dictionary.

### 3. [Severity: **Medium**] [NEW] Figure 5 Panel B places three non-comparable bars on one axis, and omits the comparator that matters

The chart shows: Freeway Network 10.3¢ · Fixed-Guideway Transit 94.7¢ · Peak Bottleneck Relief 45.0¢.

Three separate problems:
- **Bar 2 is mislabeled.** It says "Fixed-Guideway Transit," but $0.9467 is computed from *all* UTA capital
  divided by *all* UTA PMT — bus, vanpool, and paratransit included. It is not a fixed-guideway number.
- **Bar 3 is a different scope silently placed on the same axis.** 45.0¢ is the favourable endpoint of the
  unsourced `$0.45–$0.85` peak-period range (`AUDIT.md` B13). It is peak-hour, bottleneck-specific, and an
  estimate — versus two 25-year system-wide averages. Nothing on the chart indicates this.
- **The comparator is missing.** The highway peak-bottleneck figure — `$1.40–$2.20`, i.e. **140–220¢** — is the
  only number that makes transit look competitive, and it is the one bar not drawn. Omitting it means the chart
  reads as "highway 10¢, transit 95¢, bottleneck relief 45¢," implying bottleneck relief is an alternative to
  transit when bar 3 *is* transit.

A quick reader takes away the opposite of the paper's own argument.

**Fix:** split into two panels — system-wide averages (2 bars) and peak-bottleneck marginal (highway 140–220¢ vs
transit 45–85¢, drawn with error bars and a source note) — or drop Panel B's third bar entirely.

## Reasonableness / Red-Flag Scan

**Round-number red flag — [NEW] and it points at the RQ3 denominator.** Per-column divisibility:

| Column | Integer | ÷100 | ÷100,000 |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: |
| `transit_pmt_total` | 25/25 | 25/25 | **24/25** |
| `transit_upt_total` | 25/25 | 0/25 | 0/25 |
| `transit_vrm_total` | 25/25 | 0/25 | 0/25 |
| `highway_vmt_urban_freeway` | 25/25 | 0/25 | 0/25 |

Every transit PMT value is rounded to the nearest 100,000, while UPT and VRM from the *same* "audited NTD /
ACFR" table carry full unit precision. NTD reports PMT to the unit. Mixed precision within one source table is
a signature of estimation, not extraction — an independent corroboration of `AUDIT.md` A1. `transit_pmt_total`
is the denominator of the entire RQ3 capital-efficiency finding.

**Trend continuity — two undisclosed definitional breaks.** Freeway share of urban VMT:

- 2003 → 2004: **34.2% → 42.1%** (+7.9pp in one year)
- 2012 → 2013: **41.3% → 38.1%**, driven by a **+17.8%** one-year jump in `highway_vmt_urban_total`

Both have the signature of an FHWA functional-class reclassification, not travel behavior. Neither is
acknowledged. The 2013 break sits directly inside the estimation window and feeds `d_ln_vmt_pc`.

**Other red flags checked and cleared:** no rates at exactly 0% or 100%; no negative values where impossible;
gas price, population, and employment all match their FRED source files; FRED series are natively annual so the
`groupby().mean()` is a no-op (no average-of-averages problem); all growth rates >10% are either explained events
or the flagged breaks above.

**Landmine:** `fetch_and_build_dataset.py` has `gas_price_per_gal.fillna(3.22)` — a magic default equal to the
2024 value. Not currently triggered (2024 = 3.219 is present), but it would silently fabricate data if FRED
returned a short series.

**Look-ahead bias** (skill pitfall): `d_covid` is defined ex post and appears in every CV test fold — already
noted as `AUDIT.md` B7.

**Denominator shifting** (skill pitfall): present twice — the 2004 and 2013 functional-class breaks above, and
the gross-vs-marginal PMT denominator in RQ3 (`AUDIT.md` B11).

**Not applicable:** join explosion (no joins beyond clean 1:1 Year merges — verified 25 rows in, 25 rows out),
survivorship bias, incomplete-period comparison, average of averages, timezone mismatch, selection bias in
segmentation.

## Required Caveats for Stakeholders

If any version of this is shared before the fixes land, these must be stated up front:

- **Freeway VMT, lane-miles, capital outlays, and all transit series are author-compiled estimates, not retrieved
  federal records.** Only population, employment, and gasoline price are sourced (FRED). Every elasticity and
  cost figure is conditional on unverified inputs.
- **The cumulative long-run induced-demand elasticity is not statistically significant** (+0.305, *p* = 0.230,
  95% CI [−0.193, +0.804]). Only the lag-2 coefficient is significant, and its *p*-value ranges from <0.001 to
  0.072 depending on the covariance estimator.
- **No structural break is statistically significant** once the break-date search is accounted for
  (sup-F = 3.52 at 2019, below the Andrews 5% critical value ≈ 4.09).
- **The transit cross-modal coefficient is significantly positive** (γ = +0.698, *p* = 0.010), not null. This
  almost certainly reflects shared regional-growth confounding rather than transit generating driving — but it
  is not evidence of suppression, and Hypothesis 2 is not supported.
- **The +0.331 rail ridership elasticity comes from a levels regression on non-stationary series** (DW = 0.897,
  Engle-Granger *p* = 0.720). In first differences it is +0.178, *p* = 0.147.
- **Capital cost figures are nominal, undiscounted 25-year sums**, not a 20-year lifecycle cost. In 2024 dollars:
  $0.139/PMT highway, $1.319/PMT transit.
- **The peak-bottleneck cost figures ($1.40–$2.20 and $0.45–$0.85 per PMT) have no source or derivation.** Every
  conclusion that transit is cost-competitive at the margin rests on them.
- **Model out-of-sample R² is −0.199** — worse than predicting the training mean.
- **What does hold:** the ~9× gross capital cost ratio between modes is robust to inflation adjustment (9.47×)
  and to a marginal-PMT reframing (8.40×). RQ3's directional answer stands.
